Words used everywhere below

WordWhat it means here
NeuronOne unit of the network. At every moment it holds one number between −1 and +1, called its activity. Our network has 64 neurons.
Connection (or weight)A number sitting on the wire from one neuron to another. It says how strongly the first neuron pushes the second. Big positive: pushes it up. Negative: pushes it down.

QUESTION 1

Why does the board hold the way home before any training?

"Why does my board hold the way home? Why does that matter, compared with neurons that have to be trained to store the way home in their activity?"

Short answer

The board's rule is "keep what is there, and add something new every tick". Adding something every tick is exactly what a running total of the walk needs. So the board keeps a total from the very first trip, with random starting numbers. Neurons are different: to hold a number in their activity, the loop between them has to be tuned just right, and random numbers are never tuned right. So the board gets storage for free, and training only has to teach the network to read the total and steer. An activity network has to learn to store, read and steer all at once, and in our runs it never did.

Step 1. What the fly has to remember

A fly leaves home (in our task, a food spot) and wanders. Every tick it takes a small step in the direction it is facing. To get home, it needs the sum of all its steps: that sum is an arrow from home to where it is now. Flip that arrow and you have the way home. So the memory's only job is to keep a running total of the steps.

Step 2. The board's rule is an "add" rule

Every tick, every one of the board's 4,096 numbers is updated by the same rule. In words:

new board number = old board number × (1 − erase) + write strength × write gate × (this neuron's activity now × that neuron's activity one tick ago)

Look at the first part: the old number is kept, shrunk only by the erase gate. At birth we set the erase gate to 0.007, so 99.3% is kept each tick. Look at the second part: something new is added on top. A rule that keeps the old value and adds a new piece every tick is, by its shape, a running total. Nobody trained it to be one. We wrote it that way.

Step 3. Why the pieces being added carry the heading

The piece added to a board number is "activity of neuron A now × activity of neuron B a tick ago". Every neuron's activity depends on which way the fly is facing (question 10 explains why). Think of each neuron's activity as two parts: a steady part that is about the same every tick, and a heading part that changes when the fly turns.

Take a made-up pair. Neuron S is steady: its activity is 0.5 every tick. Neuron E is an "east" neuron: +0.4 when the fly faces east, −0.4 facing west, 0 facing north or south. The board number on the wire from E to S gets, every tick, 0.07 × (0.5 × E's activity). Facing east, that is 0.07 × 0.5 × 0.4 = +0.014. Facing west, −0.014. So after 30 ticks east and 10 ticks west, it holds 30 × 0.014 − 10 × 0.014 = +0.28: the same as 20 ticks of net eastward walking. That board number is the east–west half of the running total. A second pair with a "north" neuron holds the north–south half. Try it:

One honest detail. At birth, the board adds the same amount every tick whatever the speed, so it counts time spent facing each way, not distance. Time and distance agree when speed is steady. Training later taught the write gate to write more when the fly walks faster, which turns the count into distance (that is the −0.2 to +0.8 in question 3).

Step 4. The real measurement

We built untrained networks with random numbers, let 600 flies wander for 30 seconds, and at several moments asked a measuring tool (question 2) how well the way home can be read from (a) the board, (b) the activity of the same network, (c) the activity of the twin, which is our network with the board removed.

Step 5. Why it matters for learning

To come home, a network needs three things: store the running total, read it, and steer by it. Training only sees one grade, at the end of each trip. A nudge that improves storing helps the grade only if reading and steering already work, and the other way round. When all three are missing, the grade gives no useful direction for any of them.

Meanwhile there is an easy trick: spinning in place. A fly that turns hard in circles stays near the spot where it turned around, which already lowers the grade, with no memory at all. So training slides into spinning long before a memory appears. That is what happened to every twin and every GRU: 0 of 38 runs learned without help. Our network, which starts with a working total, learned in 2 of 3 cold runs at 30 s and 3 of 5 at 10 s.

Follow-up: isn't it unrealistic that connections get storage for free?

"Why do connection strengths not also have to learn to store? Is this a natural property of synapses? It seems unrealistic that they get to store for free."

Short answer

It is not free: we built the "add" rule in. The difference is what each kind of number does when nothing acts on it. A connection strength stays put unless a rule changes it. A neuron's activity is recomputed from scratch every tick, so it keeps nothing unless the loop copies it back at almost exactly 100%. Real biology has the same split: synapse changes last seconds to a lifetime, while firing dies away in tens of milliseconds unless a finely tuned circuit sustains it. But evolution can also wire precise activity circuits, so the fair phrasing is "free for training, supplied by design".

Where the worry is right. (1) We chose the board's rule and its starting settings (write strength 0.1, write gate 0.73, erase 0.007, lid ±1); in the fly story evolution would have to supply them. (2) Evolution also builds precise activity circuits: the fly's own compass ring holds its heading in a finely wired activity loop. Our twin with 16 hand-wired counters is that kind of head start, and it learned about as often as our network (5 of 13 against 3 of 5). (3) Whether the fly stores its way home in synapses at all is unknown; most fly models hold it in activity.

What the result really says. A generic rule that every synapse could carry ("strengthen when both sides are active together, fade slowly") gives a working running total out of whatever neurons are there, with no wiring specific to the task. The counter version needed 16 neurons designed for this exact job. That generality, not free storage, is the board's advantage.

So, to your exact worry: no, the board is not trained to store. The three settings that make it a running total at birth (write strength 0.1, write gate about 0.73 open, erase 0.007 per tick) are numbers we chose before training. Training later made the storing better (writing follows speed, erasing happens at the start of each trip), but it did not have to invent storing. Activity has to invent it, and from this grade it never did, except when we built the storing in by hand (question 14).

QUESTION 2

What does a "straight-line readout" of the board mean?

"What does it mean to do a straight-line readout of the board?"

Short answer

It is a measuring tool we use from outside the network. It multiplies each stored number by its own weight and adds everything up, trying to produce the way home. We pick the weights using some flies, then test the recipe on flies it has never seen. If it works on new flies, the way home is really stored there, in a simple form. The network itself never uses this tool.

The recipe, with numbers

Say we record four board numbers at the moment the fly turns around: 0.28, −0.10, 0.05 and 0.31. The recipe gives each one a weight, for example −12, 2, 0 and 3. Multiply and add: 0.28 × −12 + (−0.10) × 2 + 0.05 × 0 + 0.31 × 3 = −3.36 − 0.20 + 0 + 0.93 = −2.63. That is the recipe's guess of "how far east is home". If the truth was −2.5, the guess was close. A second recipe, with its own weights, guesses "how far north". The real board has 4,096 numbers, so each recipe has 4,096 weights.

How the weights are chosen, and how it is scored

  1. Record the board and the true way home for 600 flies.
  2. Using only the first 300 flies, find the weights that make the guesses as close to the truth as possible. (A small extra rule keeps the weights from growing silly; the research name is ridge.)
  3. Use those weights, unchanged, on the other 300 flies. Their board numbers were never used to pick the weights, so a good guess there cannot be a lucky fit.
  4. Score it with R²: take how wrong the guesses are, compare with how spread out the true answers are. R² = 1 − (guess error ÷ spread). Perfect guesses: 1. Guessing the average home for every fly: 0. Worse than that: below 0.

Why use the simplest possible recipe? Because the network's own next step is also "multiply and add" (then squash). If a multiply-and-add recipe can pull the way home out of the board, the network can learn to do the same. A clever, curvy recipe could find traces the network itself could never use.

QUESTION 3

What does "−0.2 to +0.8" mean? Are these weights?

"You mention correlations. Are these input weights? What does −0.2 to +0.8 mean?"

Short answer

No, they are not weights. A weight is a number inside the network that training changes. A correlation is a measurement we take from outside, by watching the network work. We watched two things every tick: how open the write gate was (0 = closed, 1 = fully open) and how fast the fly was walking. The correlation scores how much they rise and fall together, from −1 to +1. Before training it was about −0.2 (slightly more writing when walking slowly, an accident of the random numbers). After training it was +0.8: the gate opens wide when the fly walks fast. That means each step is written in proportion to its length, so the board counts distance, like a car's odometer, instead of time.

Why this is a nice result. We never told the network about speed. The world gives it speed as one of its four inputs, and training discovered that writing more on fast ticks makes the total more accurate. A real path integrator must do the same.

Where weights come in. The write gate is computed from the neurons through a row of weights (plus a squashing function). Training changed those weights. The correlation is how we see that the change made the gate follow speed; it is the result, not the knob.

QUESTION 4

How do you "ask the neurons where home is" early in training?

"How do you ask a neuron where home is? You said you did that early, at iterations 0, 100 and 200. How do I picture that?"

Short answer

We do not ask one neuron. We take all 64 neurons' activity at once and use the same straight-line readout as in question 2 to guess the direction home. We do this on snapshots of the network saved during training: before any training (iteration 0), after 100 rounds, and after 200 rounds. The snapshot is frozen, so measuring it does not change it.

Why the direction only, not the distance? Steering needs "which way" first. A network that knows the direction home can already turn toward it; knowing how far matters later.

Why early? We wanted to know if a run's fate could be seen at the start. If the neurons already point home at iteration 0 to 200, training has something to build on. Across all 124 of our learnability runs (every design, 10-second task), this is what we found:

At birth, before any training, the numbers were: transformer 0.82–0.84, our network 0.73–0.79, twin 0.36–0.40 (activity, 10 seconds, three starts each).

QUESTION 5

Why did the network learn to erase at the start of each trip?

"Why did it learn to erase at trip start? That seems almost correct, and probably an artifact of our design."

Short answer

You are right on both counts. Wiping the board when a new trip starts is the correct behaviour: the old trip's total is useless, even harmful, for the new trip. But the way the network knows a trip is starting is a shortcut our simulation gives it. At the start of every trip the simulation sets all 64 neurons' activity to exactly zero. An all-zero state never happens at any other time, so it is an easy signal: "if everything is zero, erase". A real fly's neurons are never wiped between trips, so a real fly would need a real cue, such as tasting food or a reward signal.

How we found out. In level 3 we believed the network erased "at food". While writing the essay, we measured more carefully and saw that arriving at food triggers almost nothing (0.005). Every trip starts on food, so the grade could never tell "erase at food" apart from "erase at trip start". We corrected every page that said "at food".

What it would take to make it honest: stop wiping activity between trips, so the only cue left is the food input. Then we would learn whether the network can find the food cue on its own. We have not run that yet.

QUESTION 6

The techniques that worked, and what they did

"Explain the techniques that worked for us, like shifting the erase gate's bias: what they did and how."

Short answer

Five settings mattered. (1) The gates start mostly shut on erasing and mostly open on writing, so the board can build up at all. (2) Moving the erase gate's starting point up made it able to learn, about ten times faster. (3) Training on short trips first (10 s), then 20 s, then 30 s. (4) Taking smaller learning steps as training goes on, so a good network is not knocked over. (5) A small charge for erasing, so the network does not erase for no reason.

First, what a gate is

A gate is a number between 0 and 1 that the network computes every tick. Inside, it first adds up a weighted sum of the neurons plus a fixed extra number called the bias. Call that total z. Then a squashing curve, the sigmoid, turns z into a number between 0 and 1. Some values: z = −5 gives 0.007; z = −2.5 gives 0.076; z = 0 gives 0.5; z = +1 gives 0.73. The bias sets where the gate sits when the neurons have nothing to say. That is its resting point.

Technique 2, the erase bias shift, explained

Training changes z in steps. But the gate's output only moves by (step in z) × (slope of the sigmoid where z sits). The slope is the steepness of the curve. At z = −5 the curve is almost flat: slope 0.007. Every lesson that reaches the gate is multiplied by 0.007, which is almost nothing. The gate was deaf. At z = −2.5 the slope is 0.07, ten times more. At z = 0 it is 0.25. So we added 2.5 to the erase gate's bias of an already trained network: that moved it onto the steeper part of the curve where lessons reach it. Drag the slider:

What happened when we did it. On a trained network (run 7B), erasing away from the trip start fell from 0.080 to 0.011 per tick within 100 iterations: the gate was finally learning. After four rounds of shifting and training (run 14), the gate fired 0.37 on average and 0.78 at its peak at the start of a trip, against 0.01 elsewhere. That is the erase-at-trip-start behaviour of question 5.

Where it failed. The same shift on a brand-new network (runs 9 and 9B) was a disaster. With no memory to protect yet, erasing had no benefit, so every lesson pushed the gate shut again (down to 0.0001, deaf again). Worse, the strong starting fade wiped the running totals before they could form. Lesson: memory first, then the reset. The shift is a tool for networks that already work.

QUESTION 7

We learned a gentle fade. Why doesn't that hurt the way fading hurts activity?

"It seems like we learned to have a gentle fade. Why does that not hurt us like it does in activity?"

Short answer

It does hurt a little, and we measured it. But the board's fade is a different kind of loss. It is small (about 0.5% per tick), it is the same for all 4,096 numbers, and it only shrinks them. The running total keeps its shape: recent steps count fully, old steps count a bit less, and nothing gets mixed up. An untrained activity loop loses information fast, unevenly, and by mixing neurons into each other, which scrambles the total instead of just shrinking it.

The numbers of the board's fade

The trained network erases about 0.005 per tick away from the trip start (the red line in question 5). Each tick, every board number keeps 99.5%. After 10 seconds (100 ticks), a step taken at the start still counts 0.995100 = 61% as much as a fresh step. After 30 seconds (300 ticks): 0.995300 = 22%.

Why a shrink is gentle and a scramble is not

Picture the total as an arrow. The board's fade multiplies every part of it by the same 0.995, so the arrow gets a little shorter each tick but points the same way. A network reading it can learn "the real distance is a bit longer than it looks". Now picture activity with random connections: each tick, each neuron's number is replaced by a mix of other neurons' numbers. The arrow is not just shortened; its parts are shuffled into each other, and after a few seconds there is nothing left to read. The real measurement in question 1 shows this: at 30 s the untrained board still gives R² 0.93, the untrained twin −0.06.

Where the fade does hurt (measured)

  • A 30-second standing stop. The board keeps fading while the fly stands still. It cost our network 0.6 on the exam (1.56 → 2.17). The hand-built brain barely noticed (0.72). Activity networks trained with a teacher lost only 0.05 to 0.23. Why they cope better is not settled: we first guessed their hold gates close while standing, but measured they stay about 0.6 open.
  • Long walks. On 90-second walks our network scored 4.4, against 7.3 for a fly that never steers. It still helps, but the fade and the lid make old steps count less and less.
  • How far versus which way. On trained networks the total's direction was right to within 5–8°, but its length was short by about 0.3 walking units. A fade shortens the arrow more than it turns it.

Why keep a fade at all? The network chose it: the erase gate could have gone to zero, and training kept it at 0.5%. A little fade keeps board numbers away from the lid (question 9) and clears leftovers that the trip-start wipe missed. It is a trade-off the network found worth making.

QUESTION 8

How does memory held in activity blow up or get forgotten, and why doesn't the board, with its fade and lid?

"Help me visualize what it means to store in activity and how things blow up or get forgotten. Why does this not happen in the fast-weight tally, when we know there's a decay and a lid? It seems like it should have similar issues."

Short answer

To hold a number in activity, a neuron must pass its own value back to itself every tick, through a loop. If the loop passes back a little less than 100%, the number shrinks away (forgotten). A little more than 100%, it grows every tick until the neuron is pinned at its maximum of 1 (blown up), and then it cannot register anything new. It has to be almost exactly 100%, and with random connections it never is. The board is different in three ways: its keep-rate is one number set by a gate (99.3% at birth), the same for every entry; what is added each tick is a fixed-size piece, not a percentage of what is already there, so it cannot grow on itself; and board numbers are not squashed every tick, only capped at ±1.

One neuron holding a number: try it

The fly walks east for 3 seconds, stands for 3 seconds, walks west for 1.5 seconds, then stands. The green dashed line is the true running total (how far east it is). The blue line is one neuron trying to hold that total in its activity. Its rule is: new activity = squash(loop strength × old activity + this tick's step). The amber line is one board number doing the same job: new = (1 − 0.007) × old + this tick's step, capped at ±1.

Why "a little more than 100%" is so dangerous

A loop multiplies. At 105% per tick, after 100 ticks (10 seconds) a number has been multiplied by 1.05100 = 131. At 95%, by 0.95100 = 0.006: gone. Even 99% leaves only 37% after 10 seconds. The window that works is very narrow, and with 64 neurons wired to each other it is harder still: the loop can shrink some patterns, grow others, and turn some into others, all at once. Training has to tune thousands of connections into that narrow window, using only one grade per trip.

So why don't the fade and the lid cause the same trouble?

  1. The fade is small, even and chosen. The board's keep-rate is 99.3% at birth (99.5% after training), the same for all 4,096 numbers, and it only shrinks. Activity's keep-rate is whatever thousands of random connections happen to produce, different for every pattern, and it also mixes neurons together.
  2. The lid is far away and only clips. Board numbers start at 0 and grow by at most 0.1 per tick, usually much less. On a 10-second walk few reach ±1. When one does, it simply stops growing; nothing else is disturbed. (On 30-second walks this does start to matter: see question 9.)
  3. Board numbers are not squashed. Activity goes through the squashing curve (tanh) every tick, which bends every number toward the limits. Board numbers are stored as they are. Only the sums the network computes from them get squashed.

Honest limit: the board is not perfect either. Its fade and its lid are real losses, and they explain why our network gets worse on long walks and long stops (questions 7 and 9). But they are slow, predictable losses, not a memory that is gone in two seconds.

QUESTION 9

Why add a lid at all, even to the board?

"Why would we ever add a lid, even to the fast weights? It seems like it causes issues."

Short answer

Because the board's "add" rule never stops by itself. If the fly keeps walking in one direction, the same board numbers keep growing, tick after tick. Board numbers feed back into the neurons, so very large ones pin every neuron at its maximum and the network can no longer think. During training, large numbers also make the lessons huge, and the numbers overflow the computer's range. The lid (cap every board number at ±1) is a safety rail. We measured its cost and found it smaller than it looks.

What we saw without a lid

The plainest version of the modern keys-and-values memory has no lid. In all three of its runs, the numbers grew until they overflowed (the computer reports "not a number", nan), and training stopped. Adding our lid and our small write strength (the "keys and values + lid" design) fixed that.

What the lid costs, measured

In run 19, after 25 seconds of wandering, 43% of board numbers were sitting at the lid. That sounds bad: pinned numbers cannot count further. So in run 21 we doubled the lid to ±2. Pinned numbers fell from 43% to 8%. But the error in the stored total barely moved (1.04 → 0.92 walking units at 25 s), and the exam got slightly worse (1.96 against a plateau near 1.6). So the lid was not the main source of error. The bigger error was in how far, which comes from how faithfully the write gate follows speed.

A brain has a lid too. A real synapse cannot grow without limit; it has a maximum strength. And there are other ways to keep numbers bounded: the delta rule (question 12) stops writing once the board already predicts what it sees, and a stronger fade keeps numbers smaller. Both have their own costs for counting.

QUESTION 10

What does it mean that some neurons "fire for north", if every neuron gets the inputs every tick?

"You mentioned some neurons fire for north, but inputs hit all neurons at every tick. What does that mean?"

Short answer

Every neuron does receive the heading every tick. But it receives it through its own two weights: one multiplies "how east am I facing" (the cosine of the heading) and one multiplies "how north am I facing" (the sine). The weighted sum is largest when the fly faces one particular direction, the one those two weights point to, and smallest facing the opposite way. So each neuron ends up with a favourite direction. "Fires for north" means its favourite is north. Even with random weights, 52 of our 64 untrained neurons had a clear favourite.

The arithmetic, with one real neuron

The world gives the network two heading numbers each tick: east-ness = cos(heading) and north-ness = sin(heading). Facing east: (1, 0). Facing north: (0, 1). Facing west: (−1, 0). Neuron 35 of our untrained network has weights 0.47 on east-ness and 0.32 on north-ness. Its heading input is 0.47 × east-ness + 0.32 × north-ness:

QUESTION 11

How is it true that our network is handed its heading?

"You said a fly's compass has to compute heading, but ours is handed it before training. I understand it's in the inputs, but how is that true?"

Short answer

Our simulation knows the fly's exact heading, because it is the one moving the fly. Every tick it computes cos(heading) and sin(heading) and feeds those two numbers straight into the network. So from the very first tick of the very first trip, the network has a perfect compass reading, for free. A real fly has no such input. Its brain has to build the heading itself, in a ring of compass neurons, by adding up its own turns and checking against what it sees. That is a hard job, and itself a memory. We skip it and model only the next stage: turning compass and speed into the way home.

The line of our simulation that does it (level3/world.py) builds the network's four inputs every tick:

inp = [cos(heading_seen), sin(heading_seen), speed, food]

"heading_seen" is the true heading, plus an optional slowly drifting error that is switched off in the standard runs. Nothing about this has to be learned; it is simply there.

Why this is fair for our question. We asked how the fly turns heading and speed into the way home, and whether a board is a good memory for that. The compass is a separate stage with its own research literature. Handing it over is a simplification, and we should say so every time. It also means one warning: some of our networks' early success comes from that clean compass (question 10 shows random neurons become direction-tuned for free). A fly with a noisy, drifting compass would have a harder job.

QUESTION 12

Why "connections between neurons that fire together get stronger"? And how is delta different?

"I understand the Hebbian math, but why do you say connections between neurons that fire together get stronger? How is that Hebbian, how does it relate to the math, and how is it different from delta?"

Short answer

Our rule adds (receiver's activity now) × (sender's activity a tick ago) to the connection from sender to receiver. A product of two numbers is big and positive when both are big and have the same sign. So the connection grows most when the sender was active and then the receiver was active: they "fired together", one right after the other. That is Donald Hebb's 1949 idea. The delta rule first asks the board "what did you expect the receiver to do?", and writes only the difference between what happened and what was expected. Hebb keeps adding every time a pattern repeats; delta stops once the board predicts it.

That is the whole link between the slogan and the math: "fire together" = same sign, both large = large positive product = the connection grows. "Fire apart" = opposite signs = it shrinks. "One of them quiet" = small product = little change.

The quirk. Real neurons cannot fire negatively; they fire more or less. Our activity runs from −1 to +1, so read negative as "quieter than usual". Two neurons both quieter than usual also strengthen their connection in our math. A brain would need a slightly different rule for that case.

QUESTION 13

What is a GRU?

"What is a GRU?"

Short answer

GRU stands for gated recurrent unit (Cho and colleagues, 2014). It is the standard textbook way to hold memory in activity. Each memory unit has a gate that decides, every tick, how much of its old value to keep and how much to replace with a newly computed value. With the gate shut, the unit keeps its value exactly, so a GRU solves the "loop must be exactly 100%" problem of question 8 for holding. But to add a step while holding, it still has to learn the right connections. Our "twin" is the same idea with one gate instead of two. We used a GRU with 52 units, sized to have the same number of adjustable numbers as our network (about 8,700).

What happened in our runs. Without help, 0 GRUs learned the task (every one ended up spinning). With a teacher at every step (question 16), two GRUs did learn: exam scores 2.11 and 2.98 on 30-second walks. So a GRU can hold the way home; it just cannot discover how from our end-of-trip grade.

QUESTION 14

What does "wiring neurons by hand" mean, and what is the twin with 16 counters?

"You wired neurons manually for some experiments. What does that mean, how do you do it, and how do I visualize it? What is a twin with 16 counters?"

Short answer

Normally every connection starts as a random number and training adjusts it. Wiring by hand means we overwrite some connections before training with numbers we calculated, so part of the network does a job from the first tick. In the twin with 16 counters, we took 16 of its 64 neurons and turned each into a slow counter for one compass direction (east, east-north-east, north-east, and so on around the circle, every 22.5°). Each counter adds up how much time the fly spends facing its direction, minus time facing the opposite way. Together the 16 counters hold the running total, seen from 16 directions. The other 48 neurons stayed random, and all 64 could still be changed by training.

Exactly which numbers we set, for counter number i

  1. Its favourite direction. Its two heading weights are set to (cos φ, sin φ), where φ = i × 22.5°. By question 10, its heading input is then cos(heading − φ): +1 facing φ, 0 at right angles, −1 facing away. Its weights on speed and food are set to 0 and its bias to 0.
  2. No chatter from other neurons. All its incoming connections from other neurons are set to 0, so nothing else disturbs the count.
  3. A nearly shut hold gate. The twin's hold gate decides how much of the old value to replace each tick (like the GRU gate above). We set its bias to −5.3, so it replaces only 0.5% per tick: new = 0.995 × old + 0.005 × squash(cos(heading − φ)).

Worked number. The east counter, fly facing east: squash(1) = 0.76, so each tick it adds 0.005 × 0.76 = 0.0038 (minus the 0.5% leak). After 100 ticks facing east it holds about 0.30. Facing west, it goes back down by the same amount per tick.

What happened. With 16 counters, the twin learned in 5 of 13 runs. With 2, 4 or 8 counters it learned in 0 of 12. The plain twin, never. One honest note: these counters count time, not distance (their speed weight is 0), like our board at birth.

QUESTION 15

Isn't a twin with working counters just our network? How is it different?

"What does it mean to train a twin with a working counter? Isn't that exactly what our model is? How is it different?"

Short answer

It is the same in the way that matters for learning: both start with a running total that already works. That was the point of the experiment. It is different in where the total lives, how it got there, and who controls it. Ours keeps it in 4,096 connection strengths that a general rule fills by itself, with gates the network controls. The counters keep it in 16 neurons' activity, and only because we designed them, knowing the answer. The result (twin with counters 5 of 13, ours 3 of 5) says the main advantage is having a working total at the start. What the board adds is getting it for free, without anyone designing it.

Why we ran it. Our network learned and the twin did not. There were two possible reasons: (a) something special about storing in connections, or (b) simply having a working total from the start. Why this still says something good about the board. For the fly: evolution would not need to design 16 perfect counters; a general rule like the board's produces a working total out of whatever neurons happen to be there. For AI: a design whose basic arithmetic already keeps a total is easy to train on tasks like this, without hand-tuning.

QUESTION 16

What does a teacher at every step do, and why do language models work that way?

"Help me visualize what a teacher at every step does. I understand it's contrived, but I need to understand why it helps. And show how linear attention and language models work this way."

Short answer

Normally our network gets one grade per trip, at the very end. The "teacher" adds a lesson at every tick of the outward walk: a readout must guess the way home from the neurons right now, and the network is charged for the error. So instead of one late lesson, it gets 100 lessons during the walk, each one saying "at this moment, home is over there, and you are holding it this badly". It is contrived because nobody tells a real fly where home is at every step; working that out alone is the whole job. It helps because each lesson arrives exactly when and where the storing happens, and it rewards storing even before reading and steering work. Language models are trained this way by nature: at every word of a text, the correct next word is known, so a 1,000-word text gives 1,000 lessons.

Language models, drawn the same way

Why this matters for our results. Linear attention, delta memories, Mamba and transformers were all invented and tuned in that setting, where a memory that stores well is rewarded immediately, at every word. Our task gives one late grade per trip, which is much harder. That is part of why designs that are excellent for language did not learn our task, and why our finding is about learning from a late, sparse grade, not about which memory is best at storing.

QUESTION 17

An empty board still learns. Is that a huge deal for showing fast weights help?

"It seems like a huge deal that empty boards still learn for fast weights. Is this a huge deal in showing that fast weights help?"

Short answer

It is an important result, with a clear limit. "Empty board" means our network with its write strength starting at 0: the board adds nothing at birth, so there is no running total to start with. It still learned 3 of 5 times, exactly as often as the normal network (3 of 5). The reason: write strength is a single number, and in all five runs training turned it up past 0.02 within 30 to 40 iterations. The moment it is up, the board is a running total again. So what helps is not the board's starting contents but its rule: turning up one number switches a working total on, and the grade finds that number quickly. An activity network has no single number like that. The limit: this shows the board's rule makes learning easy, not that the board is the only way (the transformer also learns).

Why it matters, for the fly and for AI

For the fly: a brain would not need to be born with its path-integration memory already tuned. A general rule plus one adjustable strength (something a chemical signal could set) is enough, and learning finds the setting quickly.

For training AI: a design is easy to train from a late, sparse grade when a working memory is only one adjustable number away from its starting point. That is a property you can check before training, and design for.

QUESTION 18

Why would keys and values + lid store the way home well but never learn to walk home?

"Why would a method like keys and values store well but not learn?"

Short answer

Because storing is only the first of three jobs (question 1). To come home, the network also has to read the total and steer by it, and those must grow before spinning takes over. A memory that works at birth raised the chance of learning to about 4 in 10 across all designs, but it never made it certain. For keys and values + lid we tried hard: even the version that starts as an exact running total (R² 0.94 at birth) learned only 2 of 16 times, no better than the same design without that start (2 of 12). What decides it happens during training, and we can see it but cannot yet explain it.

What we can see: every run first slides toward spinning

Our best guesses for keys and values, clearly labelled as guesses

  1. More parts must line up. In our board, key, value and query are all simply the neurons' activity. In keys and values, each is made by its own set of learned connections (16 numbers each, from 64 neurons). A lesson from the grade has to improve three translations at once, which all start random.
  2. Its store is not what the turn sees. At birth its store held the way home only weakly for a straight-line readout (R² 0.29–0.44 at 30 s), while the neurons' activity was middling (0.51–0.59 at 10 s). The piece that must reach the steering signal is weaker than in our network.

A side note on "stores well". The claim came from a separate memory test in which the network is told the right answer at every tick (the teacher of question 16). On that test keys + lid had the smallest error (0.73, against our board's 1.83). Because that test is artificial, we treat it as a side note, not as a main result.

QUESTION 19

Why does some memory work before any training?

"Why does some memory work when training starts? Why would it ever work before training?"

Short answer

Because in some designs the basic arithmetic already adds steps up, whatever the random numbers are. Our board adds a piece every tick by its rule (question 1). The transformer keeps a list of every step so far, and at birth its attention is spread about evenly over the list, so what it reads is the average of all steps taken, which points exactly away from home. (A readout just flips it.) Activity designs have no built-in adding; their random loops forget within seconds.

QUESTION 20

Why didn't we test penalties, regularization or other knobs?

"Why did we not introduce and test some penalties or regularizations or any other knobs?"

Short answer

We did turn many knobs, mostly in level 3 while building our network. In level 4 we deliberately kept one recipe for every design, because the question was a comparison: any extra knob helps some designs more than others, and tuning each design separately turns "which design learns?" into "which design did we tune best?". Where a knob answered a question, we added it on purpose, one at a time. There are still untested knobs worth trying, and I list the ones I would pick below.

A penalty is an extra charge added to the grade for something we dislike (for example, erasing). Regularization is the general name for such charges when they keep the network's numbers small or simple, to make training steadier.

Knobs I would try next, and why

  1. A small charge for turning hard. It attacks spinning directly, the trap every run slides into. Risk: it also makes the real turn toward home cost something, so its size has to be chosen carefully.
  2. Stop wiping activity between trips. Removes the borrowed cue of question 5 and asks whether the reset can be learned from the food signal.
  3. Training on mixed walk lengths. Asks whether the transformer's trouble on long walks is a design limit or just what it was trained on.

Each of these would be run the same way as before: one knob per run, the same knob for every design, several starts, predictions written down first.
