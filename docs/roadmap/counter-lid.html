A counter with a lid

Why the learned networks get worse the longer the fly wanders, while your hand-built brain does not. First a drawn version you can play with, then the real numbers from run 19.

What a "tally" is here

To get home, the fly has to keep a running count of how far it has gone in each direction. That running count is the tally. In the level 3 network the tally is not written down anywhere obvious: it lives in the fast weights, the 4,096 numbers the network is allowed to rewrite as it walks. Every tick, the rule adds a little to those numbers (more when the fly moves fast) and the network reads the total back out as "home is that way, about this far."

Here is the catch. Every one of those 4,096 numbers is clamped: it can never go above +1 or below −1. That is the lid. A number that is already at +1 cannot count any higher. When a tally is pressed against its lid we say it has saturated, from the same word as a sponge that cannot hold more water.

The signature of a lid: error that grows with trip length

Same sliders, but now we run every wander length from 5 to 60 seconds and plot the miss. A counter with no lid and no leak gives a flat line at zero. A lid gives a line that is flat, then bends upward at the moment the lid is reached. A leak gives a line that curves up from the start.

And the shape we predicted, measured

The level 4 stress test froze each brain and lengthened the wander, no retraining. If the tallies leak or saturate, error should climb with trip length. If they do not, it should stay flat. Both shapes appear.

Two honest notes. The stress test shows the error grows; it does not by itself say the lid is the cause. Compass drift, the small always-on erase, and simply never having trained on trips longer than 30 s could each add to it. And the twelve weights above are the twelve with the biggest slow-weight gain, not a random twelve, so the pinned share is likely higher here than across all 4,096.

What option 4 would try

Each idea maps onto one of the sliders above, which is what makes them one-knob experiments.

Raise the lid

Let each fast weight run to ±2 or ±4 instead of ±1. The counter simply has more room. Risk: bigger numbers can make the network's outputs blow up, which is partly why the lid exists.

the "lid" slider · flag --f_max

Count in smaller steps

Penalise large fast weights a little during training, so the network learns to write gently and spread the count over more numbers rather than pressing a few against the lid.

the "count per second" slider · new penalty term in the loss

More counters

Go from 64 neurons to 96 or 128. The tally can then be spread across more weights, each staying well below its lid. Costs more compute per iteration.

more room instead of a higher lid · flag --neurons

Train on longer trips

Add a 45-second stage to the curriculum. The network only ever met 30-second trips; it has never been punished for a counter that overflows at 40 s. This tests whether the leak is a habit rather than a hard limit.

the "wander length" slider · flag --stages 10,20,30,45

My guess at the order: raise the lid first, because it is the cheapest and the drawn version shows exactly what it should do. If the error curve stays bent after that, the lid was not the story and we look at the leak.
