Step one of the tiny-model plan

The Homing Exam

A fly leaves food, wanders in the dark, and walks back. This page is the whole experiment in one place: the world it walks in, what its brain is given, how the tally-mark memory fills and empties, the six dials we leave blank, and the search that fills them in. Everything below is one live simulation. Press play and read down.

1 · The world

A flat, dark table. Food sits at the center. Each trip has two halves: 30 seconds of random wandering, then the switch flips and the memory is allowed to drive the legs for up to 60 seconds. The agent never sees the food. If it gets within half a unit, it has arrived.

4 · The exam

One candidate, one hundred random trips. For each trip we note the closest it got to the food during the return. The average is the candidate's score, and lower is better. Random trips are a fair exam: you cannot get lucky on all of them, so the only way to score well is to actually keep the running sum.

Closest approach on each of the 100 trips, binned. A good brain piles up at the left edge.

Reading it. A brain with the fly's settings piles up near zero. Rotation at 0 gives a pile far to the right on every trip. Erase at 0 looks fine on the first trips and drifts as old tallies pile up. Heavy fade lands too close, because the early steps leaked away.

We never tell the candidate where home was. It only learns "you ended 7 units away, that is bad." That coarse feedback is all the search below gets to work with.

5 · The search

This is the training, and it is almost embarrassingly simple. Make 24 candidates with random dials. Score each on 12 trips. Keep the best 6. Make 18 new ones by copying the survivors and nudging each number a little. Repeat. No gradients, no GPU. It works because six numbers is a tiny space.

The result is not "did it get home." It will. The result is where the dials land, and whether that matches the fly: rotation near 180, erase near 1, fade near 0, and writing that scales with speed.

What the decision units learned

At level 2 the write and erase signals are no longer wired to speed and food. Each is a tiny unit that weighs three inputs. If it learned the fly's roles, write leans on speed and erase leans on food, and both ignore the distractor.

What makes it a paper. Run this search twenty times from different random starts. If all twenty land in the same place, that is a real answer. Then freeze the winner and stress it: trips ten times longer than it trained on, a drifting compass, two food sites. A tally board should survive all three. A network that holds the sum in activity should not.
