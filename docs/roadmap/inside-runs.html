Inside Runs 1, 2 and 3

We trained three small brains to find their way home, changing one thing between each run. This page opens them up and shows what each one learned, using the real numbers from the runs. Nothing here is a guess. Every picture comes with what question it answers, how to read it, and what the answer turned out to be. If you are new to this, start with the words below and come back to them whenever you need.

Showing:

These six numbers are the headline for whichever run is selected. Each is explained in its own section below.

Words you will meet on this page

Read these once. They are the only technical words used, and each is explained again where it first matters. The drawing shows how the first six fit together.

Drawn: two neurons and the connection between them

A connection's strength has two parts. The slow part is fixed wiring. The fast part is a tally mark that the brain writes and erases as it runs, and A says how loudly that mark is heard.

Drawn: the fly's tally board, and where it is in our brain

In the fly, the tally marks are synapse strengths from direction cells onto store cells. In our brain, they are the fast strengths F on whichever connections training gave a big A.

Neuron
A dot that holds one number, its activity, between −1 and 1. Our brain has 64 of them. Activity changes every tick.
Tick
One tenth of a second of the trip. Everything in the brain updates once per tick.
Connection
An arrow from one neuron to another. What flows along it is the sending neuron's activity times the connection's strength.
Slow strength
The part of a connection's strength that training sets and then locks. The brain's fixed wiring. There are 8,708 of these numbers.
Fast strength, F
The part of a connection's strength that changes while the brain runs, by a rule, with no training involved. This is where the fly's tally marks would live. There is one per connection: a 64 by 64 grid.
Gate
A number between 0 and 1 that the brain itself produces. The write gate says how much the fast strengths may grow this tick. The erase gate says how much they shrink.
A
A fixed number per connection saying how much its fast strength is allowed to count. Training sets it. Big A means "memory may act here."
Trip
Start at food, wander for a while, then try to get back. In these tests the wander is 10 seconds.
Score
How close the brain got to the food on its way back, averaged over many trips. Lower is better. Zero would be perfect. A brain that wanders at random scores about 2.6 on 10-second trips.
Iteration
One round of training: run some trips, measure the score, nudge every slow strength a little toward a better score. We ran up to 3,000.
Checkpoint
A saved copy of the brain at a given iteration. "Checkpoint 700" means the brain as it was after 700 iterations. We test saved copies so the test does not change the brain.
Frozen
Training switched off. The slow strengths cannot change. Only the fast strengths can.

1 · The journey: how the score changed as training went on

The question. Did training make the brain better at getting home, and was it using its fast strengths to do it?

Drawn: what "score" means

One trip: the grey path is the wander, the amber path is the return, the teal ring is the food. The score for that trip is the closest the return ever came to the ring. Average that over many trips and you have the number on the chart.

Drawn: what "iteration" means

Run 32 trips at once, measure the score, nudge every one of the 8,708 slow strengths a hair toward a better score, repeat. Each loop is one iteration. Every 100 loops we save a copy: a checkpoint.

How to read it. Left to right is time in iterations. Up is worse, down is better. The thick dark line is the score, smoothed over the last 50 iterations so it is not too jumpy. The teal dashed line at 1.5 is where we would say "it homes well." Every 100 iterations we paused and gave the frozen brain a little exam on fresh trips, twice: once as it is (amber dot), and once with its fast strengths held at zero (blue dot). If the amber dot is lower than the blue dot, the brain was using its fast strengths.
score, smoothedfrozen exam, F allowedfrozen exam, F held at zerotrips got longer (20 s)
Why "F held at zero" is the trick. If you take the same brain and forbid its fast strengths from holding anything, and it suddenly cannot get home, then the fast strengths were the memory. Nothing else changed. That is the cleanest test there is, and it is the one this whole project is built around.

What it shows. Run 1 improved from about 2.6 to about 1.9 over the first 700 iterations, and a gap opened between amber and blue: it was using F. Then it slipped back, and when the trips got longer it fell to the random level and stayed there. Run 2 never got below 2.6 and never opened a gap. Run 3 crossed the 1.5 line at iteration 804 on merit, moved to 20-second trips, and kept a gap of two to three units for the rest of the run: with F it scores about 1.9 on 20-second trips, without F about 4.5. One bad swing at iteration 1900, when the write gate briefly doubled and the tallies saturated, and it recovered within a hundred iterations.

2 · Three brains, the same 200 trips

The question. Where does the memory live? This is the exam from section 1 done properly: 200 trips instead of 64, and a third brain for comparison.

Drawn: the three brains

A and B are copies of the same trained brain. In B the memory wire is cut: the fast strengths are held at zero every tick. C has no brain at all and just wanders. All three get the exact same 200 trips.

Drawn: the three possible outcomes

Left: A low, B high, memory was in F. Middle: A and B both low, memory was in the neurons' activity instead. Right: all three high, the brain never learned to home.

How to read it. Three bars. Shorter is better. A is the trained brain as it is. B is the very same brain, same 8,708 numbers, but with its fast strengths held at zero every tick. C is a brain that does not steer at all, a random walk. The percentage is how many of the 200 trips got within half a unit of the food. If A is short and B is tall, the memory was in the fast strengths.
Why C matters. Without C you cannot tell whether "2.7" is bad. C tells you what "no memory at all" scores. Anything near C is not homing.

What it shows. Run 1: A is 1.73, B is 2.70, C is 2.58. So A homes better than random, and B does not. Only F could have made that difference. It is a partial success: only about one trip in five reached the food. Run 2: all three bars are about the same height. It never learned to home, so B and C tell us nothing further.

3 · Did it build its own compass cells?

The question. The fly has neurons that each fire for one compass direction. We never gave our brain any such thing. We only told it the heading as two numbers, cos and sin. Did some of its 64 neurons turn themselves into direction cells anyway?

How we checked. We ran a trip and, every tick, wrote down what each neuron's activity was and which way the agent was facing. Then, for each neuron, we averaged its activity separately for each of 16 slices of the compass. That gives each neuron a curve: "how active am I when we face north, north-north-east, and so on." A neuron that cares about direction has a curve with one bump. A neuron that does not has a flat curve.

Drawn: a compass cell

Fires hard facing one way, falls off to the sides, silent facing the opposite way. One bump. The measured curve below is drawn the same way, as a shape around the compass.

Drawn: not a compass cell

Similar activity all the way round. No bump. Tells the brain nothing about heading.

How to read it. During a trip we sorted every moving tick into sixteen slices of the compass by which way the agent faced, and averaged the neuron's activity in each slice. That gives sixteen numbers, one per direction. The left panel plots each number as a dot in its direction, at a distance from the centre equal to the activity: far out means "very active facing this way," close in means "quiet." The dots are joined and filled, so the shape bulges toward the directions the neuron likes. Under it, the same sixteen numbers as ordinary bars. Drag the slider to walk through all 64 neurons, sorted from most direction-tuned to least. The right panel shows all 64 at once as bars: each bar is how bump-like that neuron's curve is, on a scale where 1 is a perfect single bump and 0 is flat. The dashed line at 0.25 is our cut-off for calling a neuron a compass cell.

The same sixteen numbers as plain bars, from north round to north:

What it shows. Run 1 built 44 compass cells out of 64, run 3 built 38, and in both the first dozen are clean single bumps pointing in different directions. Run 2 has 18. That is the most fly-like thing either brain did, and nobody drew it in.

4 · Does the write gate follow speed?

The question. In the fly, the signal that says "record this step" is driven by how fast the fly is moving, so a fast step counts for more distance than a slow one. Did our brain learn the same, so that its write gate rises with speed?

What the write gate is. Every tick the brain outputs a number between 0 and 1. That number multiplies how much the fast strengths are allowed to grow this tick. At 0 nothing is recorded. At 1 a full step is recorded. If the brain wanted to record distance, it would make this number track speed.

Drawn: write follows speed

Fast steps write more, slow steps write less, standing still writes nothing. The dots below would climb from bottom-left to top-right.

Drawn: write ignores speed

The gate sits at one level whatever the speed. The dots below form a flat band. Steps are counted by time faced, not distance walked.

How to read it. Left: every tick of one trip is a dot. Across is the agent's speed at that tick, up is the write gate at that tick. If the gate followed speed, the dots would form a rising band. Right: the same trip laid out in time, speed in grey and the write gate in amber, with the moments at food shaded teal. The number under the left picture is the correlation: a single number from −1 to +1 saying how much two things rise and fall together. +1 means perfectly together, 0 means no relationship.

speed ÷ 1.7write gatefood present

What it shows. In runs 1 and 2 the dots are a flat band and the correlation is near zero: the gate does not care about speed. In run 3 the dots climb: correlation +0.94. The gate now writes more when the agent moves faster, which is the dopamine-like speed weighting the fly's anatomy suggests, discovered without being told. Runs 1 and 2 recorded how long the agent faced each direction; run 3 records how far it walked. That means the brain records how long it faced each direction, not how far it walked. On a world where speed varies from 0.3 to 1.7, that is a real error in the stored direction home, and it puts a ceiling on how well the brain can ever home.

5 · Does the erase gate fire at food?

The question. The fly is thought to wipe its tally when it reaches food, so the next trip starts clean. Did our brain learn to do that?

What the erase gate is. Every tick the brain outputs a second number between 0 and 1. The fast strengths are multiplied by one minus that number. At 0 they are untouched. At 1 they are wiped. A small number every tick, like 0.01, is a slow leak: the strengths lose one percent per tick.

Drawn: a reset

Near zero all trip, then a spike to 1 during the food moments (teal). The board is wiped exactly when the fly is fed.

Drawn: a fade

A low, steady leak the whole trip and nothing special at food. The board never fills, but the early steps of every trip drain away.

How to read it. The red line is the erase gate over one trip. The teal stripes are the moments the agent is standing at food. The up axis is a log scale: each gridline is ten times the one below, so 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1. We use it because the interesting values are tiny and would all sit on the floor on a normal axis. A reset would show as a red spike up to 1 inside the teal stripes and near the floor elsewhere.

6 · The fast strengths over one trip

The question. Do the fast strengths behave like tally marks: grow as the agent walks, change on the way back, and get wiped at food? And do they hit their ceiling?

What the ceiling is. A fast strength cannot go above +1 or below −1. That stands in for the fact that a real synapse cannot grow forever. A tally that is sitting at the ceiling cannot record another step; anything that happens then is lost. We call that saturated.

Drawn: a tally

Zero at the start. Some connections climb during the wander (the directions walked). On the return, the opposite ones climb until they balance. At food, all drop to zero.

Drawn: a saturated tally

Connections climb, hit the ceiling, and stick there. From then on the board cannot tell one step from another.

How to read it. Each coloured line is one connection's fast strength over the trip. We show the twelve connections that training weighted most, the twelve biggest values in A. The dashed lines at +1 and −1 are the ceiling. The purple dashed line is where the wander ends and the return begins. Teal is the food moment.
ceiling ±1food presentreturn begins

What it shows. In run 1 the lines do move with the trip, which is why the memory works a little, but a fifth of the time they are stuck at the ceiling. In run 2 they climb straight to the ceiling and stay there for more than half the trip; nothing erases them, so the board fills and stops recording, which is why run 2 cannot home. In run 3 the lines stay well inside the ceiling, saturated only 5% of the time, because the write gate is lower and speed-weighted: the tallies have room to record a whole 20-second wander.

7 · A: where training decided memory may live

The question. We did not tell the brain which connections should be the tally board. A is one number per connection saying how much that connection's fast strength counts. Training set it. Where did it put the memory?

Drawn: memory concentrated on store cells

A few rows are bright: those receiving neurons are the store. This is the fly's layout, where four neuron types hold the tally.

Drawn: memory smeared everywhere

No row stands out. Every connection carries a little memory. Harder to read and not what the fly does.

How to read it. A 64 by 64 grid. Each row is a receiving neuron, each column a sending neuron, so a square is one connection. Amber means positive, red means negative, and brightness is how big the number is compared with the biggest one. A bright row would mean "this neuron is a store cell: memory acts on everything coming into it." A speckled field with no pattern means memory is spread thinly everywhere.

An honest note. The average size of A hardly moved from where it started, at 0.08. So most of the pattern you see is still the random starting values, with training's changes laid on top. Training did not carve out a clear store.

8 · The two runs side by side

Every measurement on this page, for both runs, next to what a fly-like brain would show.

measurementrun 1 · 10 srun 2 · 10 srun 3 · 20 sfly-like would be
The short version. Run 1 built compass cells and a working but leaky memory in its fast strengths, records time instead of distance, and fades instead of resetting. Run 2, with the fade made expensive, never learned to erase at all, filled its tally to the ceiling, and could not home. Run 3, with two seconds at food instead of half a second, is the first to home on 20-second trips (1.99 with F, 4.75 without), and its write gate now follows speed with a correlation of +0.94: it discovered distance-weighted writing on its own. What no run has found yet is the reset: run 3's erase gate is a fade like run 1's, 0.005 per tick, with nothing special at food. Two of the fly's three ingredients, compass cells and speed-gated writing, have now appeared by themselves. The third, wiping at reward, is the target for run 4.
